A reading list on Gaussian binomial coefficients, q-binomial coefficients, and their connection to stochastic AUROC

Part 1

Quick orientation / popular sources

[👉 Wikipedia — Gaussian binomial coefficient](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) The best first overview.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [MathWorld](<https://mathworld.wolfram.com/q-BinomialCoefficient.html>) — q-Binomial Coefficient. A compact formula reference.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [NIST DLMF, Chapter 17](<https://dlmf.nist.gov/17.2>) — q-Hypergeometric and Related Functions; q-Pochhammer symbols, q-binomial coefficients, and the q-binomial theorem. Not the friendliest introduction, but very reliable for formulas.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [An Invitation to Enumeration](<https://enumeration.ca/extensions/q-analogues/>) — q-analogues; One of the clearest online introductions to the topic. This is exactly the language needed for random ROC wandering, where a path is weighted by its area.

Gentle textbook-style entry points
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [George Andrews, Kimmo Eriksson — Integer Partitions](<https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/41184/sample/9780521841184ws.pdf>) Probably the best textbook-style entry point. It has a chapter on Gaussian polynomials, lattice paths and q-binomial numbers, and the q-binomial theorem. A good source if you want understanding rather than just formulas.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [Peter Cameron — Notes on Counting: An Introduction to Enumerative Combinatorics](<https://webspace.maths.qmul.ac.uk/b.jackson/MTHM030/counting.pdf>) A useful source for q-analogues from the finite-vector-space point of view. It explains why the Gaussian coefficient, when q is a prime power, counts k-dimensional subspaces of an n-dimensional vector space over GF(q). This is not the main route for ROC, but it helps explain why the topic is so large.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [Laszlo Babai-style notes — q-combinatorics](<https://people.math.sc.edu/laszlo/Qcomb.pdf>) Short lecture notes.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [Herbert Wilf — generatingfunctionology](<https://www2.math.upenn.edu/~wilf/gfology2.pdf>) Not specifically about Gaussian binomial coefficients, but extremely useful for learning the general language of generating functions. For our purposes, the key habit is: a discrete distribution can be encoded as the coefficients of a polynomial. That is exactly what happens with the AUROC distribution.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [Richard Stanley — Enumerative Combinatorics, Volume 1](<https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781139200561_A24436694/preview-9781139200561_A24436694.pdf>) The classic serious textbook. Not a light introduction, but an excellent reference if you want to place q-binomial coefficients inside the broader world of inversions, partitions, posets, generating functions, and q-analogues.


Historical sources
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [H. A. Rothe — Handbuch der reinen Mathematik, 1811](<https://enumeration.ca/extensions/q-analogues/>) Historically important. The original is not an easy read, but Rothe is often mentioned as one of the early published sources for the q-binomial theorem.
[P. A. MacMahon — Combinatory Analysis, 1916](<https://archive.org/details/combinatoryanal01macmuoft>) A central source for the combinatorial and generating-function tradition. If your interest is “area under a path,” “inversions,” and “partitions,” MacMahon is closer to our ROC story than the purely analytic q-series tradition.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [Leonard Carlitz — A set of polynomials, 1940](<https://projecteuclid.org/journals/duke-mathematical-journal/volume-6/issue-2/A-set-of-polynomials/10.1215/S0012-7094-40-00639-1.full>)  Not the easiest entry point, but Carlitz is important in the later q-polynomial and finite-field tradition.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [Frank Wilcoxon — Individual Comparisons by Ranking Methods, 1945](<https://www.jstor.org/stable/3001968>) This is not about q-binomial coefficients, but it is important for the statistical side of the story. It belongs to the origin of rank-based methods, which are directly connected to AUROC.
[👉](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_binomial_coefficient>) [Mann, Whitney — On a Test of Whether one of Two Random Variables is Stochastically Larger than the Other, 1947](<https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-mathematical-statistics/volume-18/issue-1/On-a-Test-of-Whether-one-of-Two-Random-Variables/10.1214/aoms/1177730491.full>) The key historical source for the Mann–Whitney U statistic. AUROC can be viewed as a normalized Mann–Whitney statistic. In our setting, random tie-breaking inside score blocks gives a finite distribution of a closely related statistic.

Hard but interesting sources

👉 [George Andrews — The Theory of Partitions](<https://personal.science.psu.edu/lxv1/alg/antheory/preprint/andrews/chapter.pdf>) A classic work in partition theory. Useful if you want to understand Gaussian polynomials as generating functions for partitions. Deeper and harder than Andrews–Eriksson.
👉 [Gasper, Rahman — Basic Hypergeometric Series](<https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/EE815D520EA955C2A180BD85A3910C8C/9780511526251c1_p1-37_CBO.pdf>) also [option2](<https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/EE815D520EA955C2A180BD85A3910C8C/basic_hypergeometric_series.pdf>) The heavy analytic side of q-series. Not necessary for the ROC project at the beginning, but it is a standard deep reference if you move toward basic hypergeometric series.
